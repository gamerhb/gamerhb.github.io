When valuation methods disagree, don’t average them

In our Apple valuation project, the three headline outputs were far apart: $174.76/share from FCFF, $22.03 from DDM, and $140.55 from peer P/E.

The temptation is to treat three methods as three votes and average them. That would have missed the most interesting part of the project.

DDM was answering a narrower question

Apple returned far more capital through repurchases than dividends. FY2025 repurchases were roughly $90.7B versus about $15.4B of dividends. A dividend-discount model was therefore capturing only a small slice of the company’s payout policy.

Peer P/E was measuring relative expectations

The peer median multiple was 18.84×, while Apple’s market P/E was around 35.04×. That gap said more about the premium embedded in Apple’s market valuation than about an intrinsic value floor.

FCFF was the most complete intrinsic framework

The FCFF approach explicitly modeled operating performance, reinvestment, WACC, terminal growth, enterprise value, and the cash/debt bridge to equity value.

My takeaway: disagreement between models is often information. The question is not “which number wins?” but “which assumptions make each number sensible?”
